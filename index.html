Jannik Gut, Noah Kleinschmidt, Thomas Lemmin

Bioinformatics, Volume 42, Issue 8, August 2026

Limits of Protein Structure Prediction

Deep learning structure predictors like AlphaFold2 and RoseTTAFold have fundamentally transformed structural biology. By learning intricate spatial representations from vast protein sequence and structural databases, these models routinely predict ground-state 3D structures with atomic accuracy


Yet, biology is not static. Many proteins undergo dramatic conformational switches to perform their biological functions, transporters transitioning between apo and holo states, or viral spikes proteins flipping between pre- and post-fusion states.


When state-of-the-art predictors fail on dynamic targets, a common reflex is to blame the architecture. The narrative goes that these deep learning models simply lack a fundamental understanding of physical energy landscapes.


But in mainstream machine learning, a model's failure rarely marks the end of analysis; it should trigger systematic probing. Rather than treating AlphaFold like a static black box, we wanted to ask a more diagnostic question: Is the model actually incapable of representing alternative states, or are we just giving it the wrong inputs?

Conformation switching Bombyx mori pheromone-binding protein in holo (PBD ID: 2P71, cyan) and apo (2FJY, purple) confirmation.

Anyone who has spent hours stuck on a brutally difficult video game level knows the feeling: after failing the same boss fight twenty times, it’s easy to blame bad game mechanics or clunky physics. But more often than not, the game mechanics are fine. It is our strategy that is wrong. We are simply bringing the wrong gear.


Structure prediction falls into the exact same trap. When these models fail on complex, out-of-distribution (OOD) edge cases, we tend to blame the architecture when the real problem is just bad inputs.


To illustrate this, consider a benchmark dataset of 192 alternative conformations across dynamic proteins, a dataset previously used to suggest that AlphaFold2 struggles to find these different conformations.


Instead of accepting this performance drop as an unchangeable model limit, a principled diagnostic audit reveals a very different story when we systematically isolate data quality and input conditioning 


Data Hygiene First | A rigorous audit revealed that 18 structures in the dataset suffered from severe technical issues, including missing key residues or retracted entries. Discarding these unviable targets immediately reframes the evaluation baseline.


Input Conditioning via Synthetic MSAs | Natural Multiple Sequence Alignments (MSAs) reflect ground-state evolutionary pressure and data acquisition bias, not necessarily alternative state dynamics. By replacing natural database searches with synthetic, inverse-folded MSAs, the model recovered 150 previously unsolved alternative conformations.

What Synthetic MSAs Reveal about Model Mechanics

Synthetic MSAs should not be viewed merely as a technique to force an alternative coordinate output. Their true value lies in serving as programmable probes that expose how deep learning predictors balance external evolutionary signals against internal sequence priors. Systematically probing models with synthetic sequences highlights two foundational rules governing how these networks process information


1. Synthetic MSAs are highly effective at dictating global backbone topology, secondary structure shifts, and domain orientations. However, they struggle to control small details, such as side chain orientations, where the model's internal stereochemical priors dominate.


2. Predictions exist on a spectrum defined by sequence strength. When a protein's single-sequence prior is weak or flexible, synthetic MSAs readily steer the model into alternative minima. Conversely, proteins with exceptionally dominant sequence priors will completely reject conflicting MSA signals, predicting the exact same ground-state structure whether supplied with a good MSA, a bad MSA, or no MSA at all.

FrankenMSA: An Open-Source Probing Framework

To move computational biology toward a culture of active model probing, we created FrankenMSA. FrankenMSA aggregates state-of-the-art MSA engineering tools, including PLMSearch, MMseqs2, AFCluster, VC-MSA, Ghostfold, MSA slicing, and synthetic inverse-folded MSAs, into a single unified workspace. Available as both a Python package for developer workflows and a Colab GUI notebook for rapid testing, it allows researchers to systematically steer and diagnose model behavior on their own target proteins.

A New Paradigm for Computational Biology

Progress in machine-learning-driven biology will not come solely from building larger models or pointing out edge-case failures. It requires wide adoption of  diagnostic rigour: isolating variables, controlling for input representations, and systematic probing of latent representations, to understand model biases, limitations, and failure points. Synthetic sequences provide the control knob needed to explore these learned landscapes. Transforming how we diagnose, understand, and interact with deep learning predictors.
